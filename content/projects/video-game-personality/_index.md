**Appalachian State University · Honors thesis · December 2023**

## The research question

Do people describe their personality, body-esteem, and self-esteem differently when thinking about everyday life and social online gaming?

## My contribution

I developed and conducted a survey study with 190 undergraduate participants, reviewed the relevant literature, and analyzed paired responses using repeated-measures ANOVAs. The study used established measures of personality, body-esteem, and social self-esteem.

The work involved turning an abstract question into measurable constructs, interpreting statistical results, and explaining both findings and limitations in a full thesis.

## What the study found

Participants reported higher extraversion and emotional stability in the online-game context, and higher agreeableness and conscientiousness in the everyday-life context. Some body-esteem subscales also differed. The study did not find significant differences in openness, appearance-related body-esteem, or the social self-esteem measure.

These were differences in reported self-perception. The survey does not establish that gaming causes personality changes.

## Why the design matters

All participants answered the everyday-life questions before the gaming questions, leaving the study open to order effects. The undergraduate sample and retrospective self-reports also limit generalization. The thesis includes effect sizes and methodological detail alongside its conclusions.

This work complements my operational analytics background through measurement, research design, and careful interpretation of human behavior.
